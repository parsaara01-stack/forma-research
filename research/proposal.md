# Research Proposal — v0.3

**Working title:** *When Does Growth Pay Off? Investment Timing in Small Caps*

**Type:** prospective, quantitative, longitudinal corporate finance / empirical asset-pricing study.

## Abstract (protocol; no empirical claims)

We propose to examine how small public firms' capital needs, financing constraints, and reinvestment opportunities interact to affect durable profitability, financial distress, and subsequent equity returns. We distinguish operating performance from the financing claims on that performance and then from stock-market pricing. Nine proposed questions cover entry before earnings improve, expectations already priced in, economic conditions, profit quality, funding runway, dilution, the EPS outlook, changing index composition and the value of timing after costs. A panel linking point-in-time SEC disclosures, historic analyst expectation vintages, security total returns including delistings, and macro financing regimes will test whether financing characteristics explain subsequent operating outcomes and forecast errors, and, separately, whether those outcomes are under- or over-priced. The null hypothesis is that no incremental out-of-sample prediction or risk-adjusted return premium remains after controlling for established factors, industry, company scale and data revisions.

## Primary estimands

1. **Fundamental prognosis:** change in 12–24-month cash-generating capacity per share conditional on contemporaneously observable capital requirements and funding structure.
2. **Funding transmission:** differential investment and profitability response to externally measured funding shocks, stratified by debt maturities and lender dependence.
3. **Market efficiency:** 6–12-month returns unexplained by matched controls and suitable asset-pricing factors, following date-valid financial and expectation signals.

These are distinct outcomes. A positive operating result does not imply priced-alpha, and a return association does not prove causal mechanisms.

## Main identification challenges

- Funding constraints, credit ratings and capital investment are jointly endogenous.
- High-growth firms often choose high investment and dilution; observing poor near-term cash flow need not mean inefficiency.
- Cost of debt for outstanding instruments differs from the *marginal* cost of new finance.
- Negative EBITDA, negative equity, banking balance sheets, pre-commercial biotech and REIT distributions demand different definitions.
- Surviving-index and currently-listed-company samples omit mergers, bankruptcies and delistings.
- Shareholder value must be measured *per original share*, accounting for subsequent dilution and total-return corporate actions.

## Proposed baseline models

Start with descriptive cohorts by size, industry, profitability and financing mix. Compare OLS/GLM and parsimonious survival models to simple naïve persistence and conventional profitability/leverage baselines; only after stability, attempt regularized regression and tree-based models. Equity excess returns require benchmark-relative and multi-factor alpha with uncertainty intervals and multiple-test adjustments.

## Contribution test

We must document how this differs from investment-based asset pricing, accrual anomalies, the credit channel, underinvestment / overinvestment, net issuance anomalies, and earnings forecast bias. Novelty is a research question to be demonstrated through literature comparison, not assumed.
